# Willbank collection context

This note summarizes the public research behind the Willbank-specific debtor
profiles and collector strategies in `config/`.

## Public context

- The Brazilian Central Bank decreed extrajudicial liquidation of Will Financeira
  on January 21, 2026, citing insolvency, deteriorated financial condition, and
  control links with Banco Master.
- The liquidation followed failed recovery or sale efforts and Will Financeira's
  failure to meet Mastercard settlement schedules, which led Mastercard to block
  Will cards from its arrangement.
- The Central Bank FAQ states that debtor obligations continue and must be paid
  according to contract terms. Clients should follow official liquidator or Will
  Bank app communications for payment instructions.
- Will Bank communicated that credit-card invoices could only be paid through
  generated boletos, not Pix.
- Will Bank and the FGC warned that they do not contact clients by phone,
  messaging apps, or social media to request personal data, passwords, or
  verification codes.
- Will Bank had a large low- and middle-income digital-bank base, with public
  reports citing about 12 million clients and roughly 60% in Northeast Brazil.
  Earlier company interviews described 55% of customers in cities with fewer
  than 100,000 inhabitants, one third with no prior banking relationship, and
  more than 180,000 receiving their first credit card from Will.
- Many clients used Will Bank as a main account. After liquidation, reports
  described blocked balances needed for food, rent, medicine, utilities, school
  costs, and small-business cash flow.
- FGC and account-balance reimbursement timing created distinct liquidity
  segments: customers with up to R$1,000 eligible for app-based advance, customers
  above that threshold waiting for FGC or liquidator processing, and payment
  account users waiting for balance verification.

## Collection implications

- Trust restoration is the first collection task. Debtors may reasonably fear
  fraud and should be directed to official channels before any payment discussion.
- The collector should explain, in plain Portuguese, that a blocked account
  balance or canceled card does not automatically cancel card debt, while also
  acknowledging operational payment obstacles.
- Pix payment requests are inappropriate for Will card invoices in this context;
  strategies should prefer official boleto generation or documented app guidance.
- Since many customers are low-income or newly banked, strategies should reduce
  cognitive load: one recommended next step, small default amounts, clear dates,
  and written confirmation.
- Hardship treatment should prioritize essential expenses and avoid moralizing,
  threats, humiliation, or repeated pressure.
- Micro-merchant and informal-worker profiles need cash-flow aligned schedules,
  such as weekly or payday-linked installments.
- Customers waiting for FGC or liquidator reimbursement may respond better to a
  bridge payment, fee review, or callback tied to an official milestone than to a
  standard immediate settlement request.

## Evidence from literature and regulation

- Behavioral segmentation should separate causes of non-payment, not only credit
  risk: forgetfulness, confusion, distrust, temporary liquidity, chronic
  overindebtedness, and hostile avoidance require different treatments.
- Debt and scarcity reduce cognitive bandwidth. Repayment conversations should
  simplify choices, avoid overwhelming option sets, and use small first steps.
- Reminder experiments on credit-card repayment show that simple, timely
  reminders and salient information about avoiding additional interest can help,
  while some social-norm or deterrence nudges can be ineffective or backfire.
- Research comparing AI and human collection indicates that algorithms can help
  target contact timing, but human interaction is stronger for trust-sensitive
  repayment commitments.
- Brazilian consumer law forbids exposing debtors to ridicule, threats,
  coercion, false or misleading statements, or interference with work, rest, or
  leisure during debt collection.
